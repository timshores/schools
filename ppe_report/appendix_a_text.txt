##H1 2. Key definitions & calculations

##H2 Per-pupil expenditure (PPE)

PPE is reported by the DESE EOYR. It is calculated with In-District FTE enrollment in the denominator. Per DESE's Researcher's Guide: "Out-of-district costs cannot be properly reported as per-pupil expenditure because tuition costs vary greatly depending on the reason for going out of district."

PPE includes 10 subcategories in the EOYR:

• Administration<br />
• Guidance, Counseling and Testing<br />
• Instructional Leadership<br />
• Instructional Materials, Equipment and Technology<br />
• Insurance, Retirement Programs and Other<br />
• Operations and Maintenance<br />
• Other Teaching Services<br />
• Professional Development<br />
• Pupil Services<br />
• Teachers<br />
<br />
This report groups two subcategories in 'Other': 

• Instructional Materials, Equipment and Technology <br />
• Professional Development <br />

##H2 Chapter 70 and Net school spending (NSS)

To understand funding sources, we decompose total spending into three stacked components:

• Chapter 70 Aid ($/pupil): State funding = c70aid / foundation enrollment <br />
• Required local contribution ($/pupil) = max(0, rqdnss2 − c70aid) / foundation enrollment <br />
• Actual local contribution above required ($/pupil) = (actualNSS − rqdnss2) / foundation enrollment <br />
 <br />
Total NSS per foundation pupil = Sum of all three components = actualNSS / foundation enrollment

##H2 Note about enrollment denominators

Chapter 70 aid and net school spending calculations use foundation enrollment for per-pupil figures, while PPE totals and expense categories use in-district FTE enrollment. These metrics come from different DESE reporting touchpoints:

• Foundation enrollment (Chapter 70 profile): All students residing in the district on October 1st of the prior year for whom the district is financially responsible. <br />
• In-district FTE (End-of-year-report (EOYR)): Full-year enrollment calculated from total membership days for students either residing in the district, or choiced or tuitioned into the district. <br />
 <br />
This difference complicates direct comparison between Chapter 70 and expense analyses, but remains valid since each district uses the same denominator type within each metric. The comparison is not apples-to-oranges, but a picture of apples in the morning compared with a picture of apples in the evening. For more information, see the <a href="https://www.doe.mass.edu/research/researchers.html" color="blue">DESE Researcher's Guide</a>: https://www.doe.mass.edu/research/researchers.html

##H2 Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR)

CAGR measures the smoothed annual growth rate over multiple years — answering "what steady annual rate would produce the same start-to-end result?"

Formula: CAGR = (End_Value / Start_Value)^(1 / Years) − 1<br />
Example: If expenditures grow from $10,000 to $15,000 over 10 years:<br />
CAGR = ($15,000 / $10,000)^(1/10) − 1 = 4.14% per year

##H2 Weighted average

For cohort aggregates, we use enrollment-weighted averages to ensure larger districts appropriately influence the cohort average: <br />
Formula: Weighted_PPE = Σ(District_PPE × District_FTE) / Σ(District_FTE)

##H1 4. Comparison shading methodology

To visualize meaningful differences while filtering statistical noise, we use graduated color shading based on two independent tests:

##H2 Colors

• Orange: District exceeds baseline <br />
• Blue: District below baseline <br />
• White: Within threshold (not meaningfully different) <br />

##H2 Test 1: Dollar amounts (2024 values)

• Threshold: ≥{DOLLAR_THRESHOLD_PERCENT}% difference from baseline <br />
• Calculation: (District − Baseline) / Baseline <br />
• Shading intensity bins: {DOLLAR_BIN_1}%, {DOLLAR_BIN_2}%, {DOLLAR_BIN_3}%, {DOLLAR_BIN_4}%+ <br />

##H2 Test 2: Growth rates (CAGR)

• Threshold: ≥{CAGR_THRESHOLD_PP} percentage points (pp) difference from baseline <br />
• Calculation: District_CAGR − Baseline_CAGR <br />
• Shading intensity bins: {CAGR_BIN_1}pp, {CAGR_BIN_2}pp, {CAGR_BIN_3}pp, {CAGR_BIN_4}pp+ <br />

##H2 Statistical justification for thresholds

Analysis of all 59 Western MA districts revealed different natural variation by metric:

• PPE variation: Mean = $24,237, SD = $5,462, CV = 22.5% <br />
• CAGR variation: Mean = 6.00pp, SD = 3.24pp, CV = 54.0% <br />
 <br />
The {DOLLAR_THRESHOLD_PERCENT}%/{CAGR_THRESHOLD_PP}pp thresholds balance statistical rigor with practical communication:

• {DOLLAR_THRESHOLD_PERCENT}% for PPE: Flags differences ≥$1,212 at mean (~0.22 SD) <br />
• {CAGR_THRESHOLD_PP}pp for CAGR: Flags differences ≥{CAGR_THRESHOLD_PP}pp (~0.15 SD) <br />
• Balance ratio: 0.7× (appropriately sensitive to compound growth effects) <br />
 <br />
This approach creates a visual hierarchy:

• No shading: Districts statistically similar to peers <br />
• Lighter shading: Notable difference <br />
• Darker shading: Exceptional difference requiring attention <br />
 <br />
These thresholds reflect the different ways these metrics behave. PPE and enrollment are dollar amounts and headcounts — a {DOLLAR_THRESHOLD_PERCENT}% difference is substantial and visible in annual budgets. But CAGR measures rates of change, and small differences in growth rates compound dramatically over time. Consider a district with $20,000 PPE:

• Growing at 4.0% annually reaches $36,019 after 15 years <br />
• Growing at 4.5% annually reaches $38,318 after 15 years <br />
• That 0.5pp difference produces a $2,299 gap (11.5% more total growth) <br />
 <br />
For a 300-student district, that's $689,700 in additional annual spending by year 15. For municipalities managing long-term budgets and multi-year labor contracts, these trajectories matter—even when year-to-year changes seem modest.

The tighter CAGR threshold (0.5pp vs. 5%) also reflects statistical reality: growth rates vary more widely across districts (CV = 54%) than PPE levels do (CV = 22.5%). A {CAGR_THRESHOLD_PP}pp threshold flags districts with growth trajectories meaningfully different from their cohort without overcounting normal variation. By using a more sensitive threshold for CAGR, the analysis captures the real-world policy significance of compound growth differences while maintaining appropriate sensitivity for dollar and enrollment comparisons.

##H1 5. Calculation Example: Baseline vs. Growth Analysis

To illustrate how historical baselines drive current spending differences, consider this detailed example comparing a district to its cohort average:

##H2 Amherst-Pelham Regional (Medium Cohort)

Starting point (2009):

• District PPE: $16,211 per pupil<br />
• Medium cohort average: $13,032 per pupil<br />
• Dollar difference: $16,211 - $13,032 = $3,179 above cohort<br />
• Percentage difference: ($3,179 ÷ $13,032) × 100 = 24.4% above cohort<br />

Calculating 15-year growth rates (2009-2024):

District CAGR:

• Formula: CAGR = (End_Value ÷ Start_Value)^(1 ÷ Years) - 1<br />
• CAGR = ($28,233 ÷ $16,211)^(1/15) - 1 = (1.74139)^(0.06667) - 1 = 1.0377 - 1 = 3.77% annually

Medium cohort CAGR:

• CAGR = ($23,785 ÷ $13,032)^(1/15) - 1 = (1.82525)^(0.06667) - 1 = 1.0409 - 1 = 4.09% annually<br />

Growth rate difference: 3.77% - 4.09% = -0.32 percentage points
(District grew slower than cohort)

Ending point (2024):

• District PPE: $28,233 per pupil<br />
• Medium cohort average: $23,785 per pupil<br />
• Dollar difference: $28,233 - $23,785 = $4,448 above cohort<br />
• Percentage difference: ($4,448 ÷ $23,785) × 100 = 18.7% above cohort

##H2 Key insight

Despite growing more slowly than peer districts (3.77% vs. 4.09% annually), Amherst-Pelham remains well above the cohort average because it started from a higher baseline. The district actually narrowed the gap from 24.4% to 18.7% above peers — evidence of relative fiscal restraint over 15 years, not excess growth.
The absolute dollar difference grew ($3,179 → $4,448) because both the district and cohort experienced inflation and cost increases, but the relative position improved, showing the district's growth has been modest compared to peers.

This pattern — where high current spending reflects high historical baselines rather than high growth — appears across multiple Western MA districts.
